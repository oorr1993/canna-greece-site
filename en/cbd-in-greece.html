CBD in Greece — what changed in 2026

Updated: August 2026 · Reading time: 5 minutes

In May 2026, Law 5302/2026 came into force, under which the Greek medicines regulator (EOF) banned the retail sale, distribution and supply of dried hemp flower to consumers — regardless of its THC or cannabinoid content. Other CBD products, such as oils, cosmetics and supplements, retain a lawful route to market.

If you have read a travel guide recommending a CBD shop in Greece, it was probably written before May 2026 and no longer describes the position. This page explains exactly what changed, what is still permitted, and why it matters if you are travelling.

What exactly was banned

The ban targets one product: dried hemp flower sold to the end consumer. It is not a ban on the cannabinoid CBD as such, and not a ban on the hemp industry as a whole.

The critical point is that the ban is not concentration-dependent. Until 2026, the prevailing logic across Europe was that hemp flower containing up to 0.2% THC counted as a lawful industrial product. Law 5302/2026 breaks that logic where retail sale is concerned: dried flower sold to consumers is prohibited, even where the THC content is negligible.

The reasoning behind that line, from a regulator's point of view, is that it is difficult to distinguish "industrial" flower from ordinary cannabis visually or in routine enforcement. Rather than rely on laboratory testing in every shop, Greece removed the category from the shelf entirely.

Note the last row: medical cannabis was not touched by this ban at all. These are two entirely separate regulatory routes running in parallel without colliding. More on that below.

Why Greece did this

The CBD flower market in Greece grew quickly in the years before the ban, particularly around tourist centres. From the regulator's perspective this created an awkward situation: a product that looks, smells and is consumed exactly like cannabis was being sold in open shops, while cannabis itself remained a criminal matter and medical cannabis was placed under tight pharmacy supervision. The ban straightens that line.

The decision was not uncontested. A Greek government advisory body warned that the ban may conflict with EU law on the free movement of goods. In parallel, comparable questions were referred to the EU Court of Justice in an Italian case, and a ruling is not expected before late 2026. The position may therefore change again.

But that is not much use to a traveller right now. A future legal argument is no defence on the ground. What governs today is the Greek law in force, and the safe assumption is that it is being enforced.

What it means for a visitor

In practice: the route that until this year was the simplest — walk into a shop in Greece and buy CBD flower with no prescription and no questions — is no longer lawfully available.

Three practical points:

  • Older guides are misleading. Most travel content about CBD in Greece was written between 2023 and 2025 and describes a thriving shop market. It has not been updated.
  • An open shop is not evidence of legality. Enforcing a new ban takes time, and you may well find shops still selling. That does not make the purchase lawful, and it certainly does not protect the buyer.
  • The confusion can be costly. Someone who buys flower assuming it is "only CBD" and carries it in a pocket is in fact carrying a substance that looks like cannabis, with no identifying packaging and no prescription. If they are checked, they have no document supporting their account.

Bringing CBD into Greece

This is not a decision to make on your own on the strength of a travel guide. A product sold lawfully in one country is not necessarily lawful in another, and the label on the packaging is not decisive — customs authorities test actual content, not manufacturer declarations.

The complexity is twofold: both the export rules of your home country and the import rules of Greece apply to the same journey, and they are not identical. Anyone considering bringing a hemp product should check in advance with the competent authorities on both sides, rather than rely on the fact that it was bought lawfully at home.

The general principle set out on flying to Greece with medical cannabis applies here too: a document issued in your home country, including a notarised translation, is not a Greek permit.

Frequently asked questions — CBD in Greece

Is CBD legal in Greece in 2026?

Partly. Law 5302/2026, in force since May 2026, bans retail sale of dried hemp flower to consumers regardless of THC content. Oils, cosmetics and supplements retain a lawful route.

Can I still buy CBD flower in a shop in Greece?

No. The ban covers retail sale to consumers, and the Greek medicines regulator (EOF) enforces it through market inspections. A shop that continues to sell flower is not operating lawfully in respect of that product.

What about CBD oil?

Oils were not covered by the flower ban and retain a lawful route to market, alongside cosmetics and food supplements. The ban is directed at dried flower only.

What is the difference between CBD and medical cannabis in Greece?

Two separate routes. CBD was sold as a consumer product without a prescription — that is the route restricted. Medical cannabis is prescribed by a Greek-licensed doctor, sold only at licensed pharmacies, and its status has not changed.

Can I bring CBD products into Greece from abroad?

Do not assume you can. A product lawful at home is not necessarily lawful in Greece, customs test actual THC content, and the label is not decisive. Check in advance with the competent authorities on both sides.

Could the ban be overturned?

Possibly. A Greek government advisory body warned of a conflict with EU law, and comparable questions were referred to the EU Court of Justice in an Italian case — but a ruling is not expected before late 2026. Until then the Greek law in force governs.
